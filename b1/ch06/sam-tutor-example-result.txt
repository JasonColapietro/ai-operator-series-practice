**1. The main idea**

Every transaction changes at least two accounts, and the books always stay balanced: Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity.

Debit and credit only tell you which side of an account you write on. Debit is the left side. Credit is the right side. Neither one means "good" or "bad," or "in" or "out."

Whether the left side makes an account go up or down depends on the type of account. Use DEALER. Drawings, Expenses and Assets go up with a debit. Liabilities, Equity and Revenue go up with a credit. To make any account go down, use the opposite side.

In every entry, total debits equal total credits. So if Cash goes up by $100 (a debit), some other account gets a $100 credit.

**2. Your sticking point**

Your instructor's hint is the answer: your bank statement shows the bank's books, not yours. To the bank, the money in your account is money it owes you, so it's a liability on the bank's books. When you get paid, the bank owes you more, and liabilities go up with a credit, so the bank "credits" your account. When you swipe your debit card, the bank owes you less, so it debits that liability. On your own books, that same money is Cash, an asset, and assets go up with a debit. Same money, opposite sides, because it's your asset and the bank's liability.

**3. A worked example**

*Oct 1: A dog-walking business borrows $2,000 from the bank.*

Step 1: Which accounts change? Cash (the business gets money) and Loans (the business now owes the bank).

Step 2: What type is each? Cash is an asset. Loans is a liability.

Step 3: Up or down? Both go up. The business has more cash and owes more.

Step 4: Debit or credit? Assets go up with a debit, so debit Cash. Liabilities go up with a credit, so credit Loans.

Step 5: Check. Debits $2,000 = credits $2,000. The equation stays balanced: assets up $2,000, liabilities up $2,000.
